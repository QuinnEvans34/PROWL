# BSAAI Curriculum Coverage Reference

## Purpose

This document summarizes the material represented across Neumont University's Bachelor of Science in Applied Artificial Intelligence and Data Engineering program. Its primary purpose is to provide durable context for planning and evaluating a capstone project proposal.

The document should help an AI model, student, instructor, or reviewer answer the following questions:

- Which major technical and professional disciplines are represented in the degree?
- Which concepts, methods, and practices could reasonably be demonstrated through a capstone?
- Does a proposed project integrate enough of the program to function as a capstone rather than an ordinary course project?
- Which project artifacts can serve as evidence that a competency was actually demonstrated?
- Which areas are central to the proposed project, which are supporting areas, and which are outside its reasonable scope?

This is **not** a capstone proposal and should not be treated as one. It is a curriculum and competency reference that can be supplied as context when writing the proposal.

### Source-status convention

The document uses two levels of material:

- **Official curriculum content**: Program outcomes, course names, credit requirements, and topics explicitly stated in official course descriptions.
- **Synthesized planning content**: Detailed topic lists that consolidate course titles, official descriptions, common course objectives, and material the student has reported covering. These details are intended for proposal planning and should not be represented as verbatim catalog language.

A course title by itself does not prove that every possible topic listed beneath it appeared in every section or every year. When exact proof is needed, use the course syllabus, assignment descriptions, repository history, or completed project artifacts.

### Catalog credit discrepancy

The official program page contains an internal accounting inconsistency:

- The summary lists **45 general education credits** and **128 program-core credits**.
- The detailed sections list **47 general education credits** and **126 program-core credits**.
- Both versions total **180 credits** after adding the seven other required credits.

This discrepancy does not materially change the competency map, but it should not be silently corrected in formal academic documentation.

# 1. Official Program Intent

The BSAAI program is designed around the practical use of artificial intelligence, data science, data engineering, software, cloud services, project management, and business analysis to solve real-world problems.

The program prepares students for roles such as:

- AI specialist
- Data engineer
- Machine learning engineer
- Data analyst
- AI project manager
- Business-oriented AI practitioner
- Technical project contributor
- Applied data and automation specialist

The curriculum combines lectures, discussions, presentations, labs, assignments, individual work, group work, and supervised real-world projects.

---

# 2. Official Program Outcomes

Graduates of the BSAAI program are expected to be able to:

1. Demonstrate organizational proficiency and optimization in the application of AI tools and platforms.
2. Demonstrate the application of machine learning models and algorithms.
3. Understand and apply cloud-based AI services within a business framework.
4. Apply data management techniques to extract, transform, load, and analyze data.
5. Leverage project management techniques to align and optimize workflow.
6. Assess the ethical implications of AI deployments in business and technology.
7. Understand emergent AI trends, predictions, tools, and challenges.
8. Demonstrate effective communication, problem-solving, and critical-thinking skills.
9. Demonstrate professionalism in communication, appearance, hygiene, and demeanor.
10. Use technical and nontechnical expertise in team-based, collaborative environments.
11. Produce professional-quality specifications, models, and other documents.
12. Apply course objectives effectively in personal and professional settings.

These outcomes should be treated as the highest-level approval criteria for a capstone. Individual courses contribute evidence toward one or more of these outcomes.

---

# 3. Capstone Standard

The official `PRO390 Capstone Project` description states that the project must demonstrate overall attainment of the learning outcomes of the student's academic program. It may be completed individually or in a team, must be approved by the instructor, and should integrate existing skills while creating opportunities to develop additional skills.

A credible BSAAI capstone should therefore do more than demonstrate a single isolated feature. It should combine multiple disciplines into one coherent system, investigation, workflow, or product.

## Practical capstone interpretation

A strong proposal should normally identify:

- At least **three primary competency domains** demonstrated deeply.
- Several additional supporting domains.
- Concrete project artifacts that prove each claimed competency.
- A realistic ten-week implementation boundary.
- Measurable technical or user-facing success criteria.
- Risks, limitations, and ethical implications.
- A clear reason that AI, machine learning, analytics, or data engineering is necessary rather than decorative.

The proposal does **not** need to force every course into one project. Artificially adding unrelated features weakens the architecture and creates unrealistic scope.

# 8. Cross-Course Themes the Capstone Should Integrate

The proposal model should look for meaningful integration across these themes.

## 8.1 Problem to system

1. Identify a real problem.
2. Define users and stakeholders.
3. Write requirements.
4. Model the information.
5. Select an architecture.
6. Implement the system.
7. Test it.
8. Evaluate its value.
9. Document risks and limitations.
10. Present the result professionally.

## 8.2 Data lifecycle

1. Identify data sources.
2. Obtain data legally and reliably.
3. Validate and clean data.
4. Transform and integrate data.
5. Store data appropriately.
6. Analyze or model the data.
7. Present results.
8. Monitor quality.
9. Govern retention, privacy, and access.

## 8.3 Machine-learning lifecycle

1. Frame the task.
2. Define labels and features.
3. Establish a baseline.
4. Split data correctly.
5. Train models.
6. Evaluate models.
7. Analyze errors.
8. Integrate inference.
9. Monitor performance and drift.
10. Document limitations.

## 8.4 Software lifecycle

1. Define scope.
2. Design components.
3. Implement in modules.
4. Use version control.
5. Test at multiple levels.
6. Package and configure.
7. Deploy or provide a reproducible run process.
8. Log and monitor.
9. Fix defects.
10. Maintain documentation.

## 8.5 Responsible-AI lifecycle

1. Identify affected stakeholders.
2. Classify data sensitivity.
3. Identify potential harm.
4. Limit access.
5. Define human-review boundaries.
6. Evaluate bias and error.
7. Document model limitations.
8. Monitor use.
9. Provide a failure and incident process.
10. Avoid claims not supported by evidence.

# 9. Capstone Coverage Test

An AI model reviewing a proposal should not merely count technologies. It should check whether the proposal demonstrates integrated competencies.

## 9.1 Primary domains

The proposal should identify approximately three to five primary domains, such as:

- Data engineering
- Machine learning
- Analytics and visualization
- Natural language processing
- Software application development
- Cloud-based AI
- Database and persistence design
- Computer vision

A primary domain should have:

- A substantial deliverable
- Technical decisions
- Evaluation criteria
- Visible evidence in the final project

## 9.2 Supporting domains

The proposal should normally include several supporting domains:

- Systems analysis
- Project management
- Quality assurance
- Ethics and governance
- Communication
- Business analysis
- Cloud or deployment planning
- Information modeling

Supporting domains may appear through documentation, architecture, evaluation, and process rather than through separate product features.

## 9.3 Weak capstone warning signs

A proposal is probably too weak when it is primarily:

- A thin interface around a language-model API
- A static dashboard with no meaningful data pipeline
- A CRUD application with AI terminology but no evaluated AI component
- A notebook without a usable system or reproducible process
- A model trained once without baseline comparison or error analysis
- A project with many named services but no clear reason for using them
- A collection of disconnected features added only to mention more courses
- A project whose success cannot be measured
- A project whose data source, legality, or reliability is undefined
- A project with no testing, governance, or failure analysis

## 9.4 Strong capstone characteristics

A strong proposal usually includes:

- A specific real-world problem
- Defined users
- A coherent architecture
- A nontrivial data lifecycle
- A justified AI or machine-learning component
- Reproducible processing
- Appropriate persistence
- Testing
- Quantitative evaluation
- Governance and ethical analysis
- Business or operational value
- Professional documentation
- A realistic ten-week scope

# 11. Proposal Review Questions

When this file is provided to an AI model alongside the repository and capstone proposal template, the model should answer the following questions before finalizing the proposal.

## Project substance

- What real problem does the project solve?
- Who uses it?
- What makes it larger or more integrated than a normal course assignment?
- Which parts already exist in the repository?
- Which parts are new capstone work?
- Is the new work substantial enough for the available term?

## AI and data substance

- What data enters the system?
- How is the data cleaned, validated, transformed, and stored?
- What AI or machine-learning component exists?
- Why is that component appropriate?
- What baseline will it be compared against?
- How will it be evaluated?
- How are incorrect, uncertain, or unsafe outputs handled?

## Engineering substance

- What are the major components?
- How do they communicate?
- What database or persistence layer is used?
- How is configuration managed?
- How is the system tested?
- Can another person run it from the documentation?
- What are the deployment and scaling assumptions?

## Governance substance

- What data is sensitive?
- What users or stakeholders could be affected?
- What ethical risks exist?
- What human-review boundaries are required?
- What claims must the system avoid?
- How will failures be logged and handled?

## Approval fit

- Which official program outcomes are directly demonstrated?
- Which courses provide the foundation for the work?
- What artifact proves each claimed area?
- Are at least three major domains demonstrated deeply?
- Does the schedule prioritize the core technical risk?
- Is the scope achievable in ten weeks?
- Are optional stretch goals separated from required deliverables?

# 12. Instructions for an AI Proposal Writer

When using this document as context:

1. Do not paste the entire curriculum into the proposal.
2. Select the competencies that genuinely match the repository and planned work.
3. Cite specific project components as evidence.
4. Distinguish existing functionality from new capstone development.
5. Avoid claiming that a technology demonstrates a competency merely because it is installed.
6. Tie each primary competency to a deliverable and evaluation method.
7. Include governance, testing, documentation, and project management even when they are not visible product features.
8. Do not force unrelated subjects into the architecture.
9. State explicitly which material is outside scope.
10. Write the proposal around a coherent project, not around a checklist of course names.
